Can you build a computer out of arrows? 5 games that run on real science

Covers Nov 1 → Nov 10, 2026 · 5 games

Steam's Programming tag got more than ten new games this week. Three of the ones that truly put tech into the mechanics do the same thing: build a computer from nothing, using arrows, assembly and marbles. The other two turn arithmetic into a production line, and make math the only language you share with aliens.

Science in the rules is what we look for. Why?

01

Logic Arrows

Digital logic · computer architecture

What you do

An infinite sheet of grid paper. Each arrow you place does one thing: pass the signal one cell in the direction it points. Line up a few dozen and an AND gate lights up; keep stacking and you get adders, memory, and finally a CPU that runs Tetris. The community has already written its own assembler inside it.

The science

Digital logic and computer architecture. It splits logic gates one level further down, using the arrow's passing rule as the only building block, and stacks from NOT and AND up to a clock, RAM and an instruction set. It plays more like a cellular automaton.

Where to play

Platform
PC (Windows / SteamOS / Linux), browser
Price
No price shown at writing (browser version: to be checked)
Demo
Free demo on Steam
Status
Full release scheduled for Nov 10

Our take

The lowest barrier to seeing how a computer computes 1+1, because there is only one rule. The road to the CPU is steep, so try the free demo first and make sure you won't quit halfway.

02

PlayASM

Assembly · CPU simulation

What you do

On the left, a code editor with syntax highlighting and breakpoints; on the right, 16 registers and memory shown in hex, decimal and binary. You write assembly line by line, step once, and watch flags flip and memory change. Then the same instructions draw on a 320×200 screen, play 8-channel chiptune, or drive a six-axis robot arm that loads blocks onto a conveyor.

The science

A custom minimal CPU emulator (16 registers, 64 KB address space, flags). 70 tutorial missions go from bits, hex and shifts to loops and the stack, plus 100+ challenges. Mods are JSON + Lua and can go on Steam Workshop.

Where to play

Platform
PC (Steam)
Price
US$15.99, 15% off until Nov 19
Demo
None
Status
Out Nov 5, no reviews yet at writing

Our take

For people who took computer organization but never got to touch a CPU; complete beginners will stall in the first few missions. There are already plenty of assembly puzzle games; this one gives you a whole machine. Pricey with no demo, so wait for the first reviews.

Sources: Steam page

03

Number Machine: Math Factory

Arithmetic · automation

What you do

Balls with numbers roll across a hex map. You lay conveyors and place pushers, lasers, magnets and rotators so two 3s collide into a 6, then line up a row of balls in the shape and values the puzzle asks for. Everything runs on its own; miss one cell and the whole line jams.

The science

Math plus automation. Machines merge, split and modify the numbers on the balls, so a formula becomes a production line. Leaderboards rank size and speed, the classic space-versus-time trade-off from algorithms class.

Where to play

Platform
PC (Windows / macOS / SteamOS / Linux)
Price
US$14.99, 10% off until Nov 20
Demo
Free demo
Status
Out Nov 6, all 23 reviews positive at writing

Our take

If you played Opus Magnum or Infinifactory, you'll be at home right away; if factory games aren't your thing it may feel like doing sums at work. The developer cites those two as inspiration; hex cells plus number balls is a variation, but a very complete one.

Sources: Steam page GamingOnLinux

04

marblebuilds

2D physics · digital logic

What you do

Stack blocks on a grid to build a marble run, press start, and watch the marble roll through boosters, anti-gravity pads, portals and slow honey zones. Then wire up buttons and sensors so a passing marble triggers trapdoors, lights or marble spawners. The developer says you can build pinball tables and calculators without writing code.

The science

2D rigid-body physics (Box2D) plus a Minecraft-redstone-like logic system: wires, logic gates, timers and memory cells. The marble itself is the signal, so physics and logic share one map.

Where to play

Platform
PC (Windows / SteamOS / Linux), web (itch.io)
Price
US$9.99, 25% off until Nov 16; web version free
Demo
Free web version
Status
Out Nov 2

The web version has sandbox, puzzle and platformer modes (logic blocks: to be checked).

Our take

The only pick this week that has Traditional Chinese and plays straight in the browser, so the barrier is lowest. If you want to study logic circuits seriously it will feel like a toy, but for seeing a circuit move first, it is the most intuitive.

Sources: Steam page Free web version on itch.io

05

Message From Aliens

Mathematics · decoding

What you do

The whole screen is alien symbols, menus and dictionary included. Each symbol has a “?” under it where you write notes, and your note appears everywhere that symbol shows up. Chapter one is all equations: you guess their number system from addition, subtraction, multiplication and division, then use what you know to unpack dictionary entries that explain alien words with more alien words. Even quitting the game has to be decoded first.

The science

Math as a shared language. Over 1,000 ideographic symbols and 20,000+ words of text build rules up from numbers and equations, later reaching ideas like gravity and electrolysis of water. The developer cites Story of Your Life and Contact as inspiration.

Where to play

Platform
PC (Steam)
Price
US$9.99
Demo
Free demo
Status
Out Nov 1, 498 reviews, 93% positive at writing

No human text in the game; you take notes in your own language.

Our take

A must if you loved Chants of Sennaar, and it is harder and purer. No art, no music, no tutorial, so if that sounds dry, try the demo. The developer previously made A=B and Understand.

Sources: Steam page 4Gamers (Traditional Chinese) AUTOMATON (Japanese)
