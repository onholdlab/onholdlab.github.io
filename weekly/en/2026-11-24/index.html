Can you sort scrap with magnets and heat? 3 games that run on real science

Covers Nov 13 → Nov 24, 2026 · 3 games

All three games this week are on Steam, and all three have free demos. The most unusual one turns a single question into the whole game: how do you separate scrap that arrives mixed together? The answer runs on magnets, melting and other material properties. Of the other two, one picks up the build-a-computer thread from issue 2 and takes it toward parallel computing, and one puts machine-learned drivers on a race track.

Science in the rules is what we look for. Why?

01

STEEL GULLET

Material physics · separation

What you do

Mixed scrap keeps pouring into your processing unit, and there are no magic filters to sort it. You chain machines together with conveyors and design your own separation line. The store page gives three examples: crush a concrete block; grind iron scrap into dust and pull it out with a magnet; melt a rubber tire and drain the liquid. Sorted materials are then reprocessed and combined into the products you owe as quotas. Space is tight, and everything is processed by mass, not by item count. The setting: Earth's orbit has shifted, it is 120°C outside, and you are an engineer on a moving ark-city.

The science

Material properties and phase changes. Separation here runs on physical properties, not filters: every material has its own temperature, density, melting point and boiling point, and you control which phase it is in. The store page is explicit that the game does not simulate how objects move; what it models is temperature, density, elasticity, solubility, melting and boiling. Crafting depends on material properties too.

Our take

The store page says it itself: there are no magic filters, so working out how to separate things is the puzzle. The press quotes on the Steam page also call it puzzle-leaning. With tight space and mass-based processing, people who want a sprawling factory to watch may find it restrictive. At US$19.99 it is the priciest game this week, so start with the free demo.

02

IT THINKS! - Build Your Own GPU

Digital logic · architecture · parallel computing

What you do

You begin with a single NAND gate, a logic gate with two inputs. You place parts, draw wires, watch the signals move, then check the design on a test bench. A working circuit can be packed into a reusable chip of your own. From there it climbs: arithmetic, memory, a programmable processor, parallel cores, matrix multiplication, and finally a chip that recognizes shapes you draw. There are 62 contracts across six eras, from a small workshop to an AI lab. If you don't want to build every part from NAND, you can license it from the factory and come back to build it yourself later.

The science

Digital logic and computer architecture, carried all the way to parallel computing. You write kernels in a compact assembly language and run the same kernel across many cores. Each design is a trade-off between area, speed, memory bandwidth, power and parallel efficiency.

Our take

After Logic Arrows in issue 2, here is another game that builds a computer from the smallest part up. What differs is where it ends: not at a processor, but at parallel cores and matrix multiplication. The store page says no electronics background is needed, with hints, short lessons and tests you can skip. At 76% positive the reviews are not especially high, so start with the demo; your progress carries over.

03

Backseat Champions

Machine learning · race strategy

What you do

The store page calls it a racing auto-battler with roguelike elements: you manage your driver's race. During a race you pick from random upgrades, spend boost and aero bonuses to attack or defend, plan pit stops, and choose among seven tire compounds that counter one another. Weather changes mid-race. A crash brings out the Safety Car and bunches the field. Maxing an upgrade at once risks an engine failure that forces a pit stop, and you decide whether to pit early or gamble on a puncture. A default race takes about ten minutes.

The science

Machine learning. The store page says the AI drivers were trained on the developer's own machines, using only the game itself as their environment and data, with traditional machine learning rather than generative AI. It also says nothing in a race is scripted and that all drivers are equally skilled, so the difference mostly comes from your decisions.

Our take

The one game this week that asks for no technical knowledge: the machine learning sits in the drivers, and your job is tire and pit calls. Good for people who want to run race strategy, with split-screen for 4 or online for up to 12. It is still in Early Access; the developer expects about two years and plans to raise the price gradually as content is added.
