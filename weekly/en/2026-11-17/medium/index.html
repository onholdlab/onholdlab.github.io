Can you draw your own transistor? 4 games that run on real science

Draw a chip layer by layer until two lines cross and a transistor appears. Or shoot numbers with division until they hit zero.

The hardest game this week goes one level below last issue's build-a-computer-from-gates theme: you draw the transistors themselves in silicon. The other three each turn a field into the rules: how traffic jams, how integers divide, how a machine watches a screen. One of them is not out until next March; we flag it so you know it exists.

ONHOLDNEWS No.03 · Nov 17, 2026 · 4 games where real science runs the mechanics

01 Circuits: VLSI

Chip layout · semiconductors

What you do

The screen is the grid of a chip. You get no ready-made gates, only five materials: metal, polysilicon, contacts, active silicon and wells. First you join two points with metal. Then you run a strip of polysilicon across active silicon, and the small patch where they cross becomes a transistor. Hit simulate and the oscilloscope beside it draws your output; it has to land inside the target band. There are 100 puzzles in 10 chapters, ending with memory, arithmetic and small clocked machines you draw yourself.

The tech behind it

Physical layout of integrated circuits (VLSI layout). A transistor is not a part you place: it forms where polysilicon crosses active silicon, and its size comes from the shapes you draw. Resistance and capacitance affect the result too, so how you route a wire changes when the signal arrives. The game runs design-rule checks, marks where you broke them, and lets you cut a cross section to see how the layers stack.

Where to play

Platform: PC (Windows / macOS / SteamOS + Linux)
Price: US$9.99, no discount at writing
Demo: ✓ Free demo (chapter one, 10 puzzles)
Status: Released Nov 6; 1 review at writing

Our take

One level below last issue's build-a-computer-from-gates games: here you draw the gates themselves in silicon. If you played Zachtronics' free KOHCTPYKTOP it will look familiar; this one adds a scope, cross sections and a 100-puzzle learning curve. The store page itself tells you to try the demo first, because it is hard.

02 Highways & Co.

Traffic engineering · flow simulation

What you do

The map has almost no buildings; stations and towns are just coloured signs marking a spot. All you do is build roads: 10 types from country lanes to four-lane motorways, a different lane count in each direction if you like, and 7 height levels for cuttings, tunnels and viaducts. You pick each junction: signals, give-way, a roundabout, or a slip road. Open the network, watch where the cars back up, change it, watch again.

The tech behind it

Traffic engineering. Every vehicle has its own route and driving behaviour; it merges, changes lanes and queues. The store page says the simulation is deterministic, so the same network does not flow one run and jam the next, and every jam has a cause you can find. To clear a site, each destination has to reach its required traffic flow, and you can set which lane connects to which and who has priority.

Where to play

Platform: PC (Windows / macOS; demo also on Linux)
Price: Full game: to be checked
Demo: ✓ Free demo (since Nov 11)
Status: Demo: 372 reviews, 97% positive at writing

Full release is planned for Q1 2027; what you can play now is the demo.

Our take

If you have ever spent a whole evening fixing one interchange in a city builder, this removes everything except the interchange. Unlike the abstract line-drawing of Mini Motorways, you manage where every single lane leads. The full game is next year, but the demo is free today.

03 Zero Sum

Arithmetic · number theory

What you do

A twin-stick shooter where the things rushing you are not monsters but integers like 36, 49 and 7, with no health bars. Your weapons are seven operators: +, −, ×, ÷, %, √ and Σ. Hit 36 with ÷6 and it divides cleanly, so your combo climbs. Hit 12 with ÷5 and it does not, so it splits into new enemies and the combo breaks. A prime like 7? Add 1 first to make it 8, then deal with it.

The tech behind it

The arithmetic properties of integers are the combat rules. Whether a number divides evenly, whether it is a perfect square, and what remainder it leaves decide if your shot is a clean kill or makes things worse. Primes will not divide cleanly, factorial enemies grow if you ignore them, and Σ pulls a swarm of small numbers into one bigger, easier target. There is also a challenge editor that exports share codes.

Where to play

Platform: PC (Windows / macOS / SteamOS + Linux)
Price: NT$268, 25% off launch week (press report); USD to be checked
Demo: ✓ Free demo (3 bosses)
Status: Listed for Nov 17; not yet on sale when we checked

Our take

Great if your mental arithmetic is fast, because spotting that 91 is 7×13 is your reaction time here. If numbers make you pause, the shooting rhythm stalls. The demo's three bosses are enough to find out which kind of player you are.

Sources: Steam page · Free demo on Steam · Gamebase coverage (Chinese)

04 Videogame Automation Engineer

Machine vision · control logic

What you do

You are a game tester who never touches the controller. On screen is a minigame, say a dinosaur that keeps running. You lay a colour sensor over the patch of screen just ahead of it, wire that to a logic block, and wire the block to the jump button of a virtual gamepad. When an obstacle enters the sensor's patch, the gamepad presses itself. Too early or too late, and you move the sensor or rework the circuit. Beating a client's high score unlocks more sensors, logic blocks and minigames.

The tech behind it

Machine vision plus control logic. Your circuit cannot see inside the game; it can only look at the screen the way a person does. Sensors report the colour of a region, whether something moved, or whether a pattern showed up, and logic blocks decide when to press which button. Automated testing by screen recognition and cameras sorting items on a conveyor follow the same idea.

Where to play

Platform: PC (Windows)
Price: To be checked
Demo: ✗ None mentioned on the page
Status: Listed for Mar 3, 2027; wishlist only for now

Our take

Worth noting for its subject: not solving puzzles with code, but teaching a machine to watch a screen, which is rare among programming games. It is the spiritual successor to the developer's JOY OF PROGRAMMING, with wiring instead of typed code. Whether it is fun, we will know when it is playable.
